# Native-language evidence

Use evidence to decide whether a phrase belongs in the target language. Fluency alone is not proof.

## Evidence ladder

Prefer sources in this order:

1. Native creators and practitioners speaking in their own words.
2. Publishers, trade publications, respected editors, and professional organizations.
3. Official terminology resources, corpora, dictionaries of usage, and language style guides.
4. Native copy from comparable products with a credible local presence.
5. Specialist communities where the target audience naturally discusses the subject.
6. General forums only when testing informal register, never as the sole authority.

Reject or sharply discount:

- translated pages presented as native evidence;
- search-engine content farms and affiliate pages;
- machine-written pages and generated summaries;
- unopened search snippets;
- raw hit counts without source quality or context;
- a dictionary definition used as proof of natural phrasing;
- sources that merely repeat the candidate phrase from the same origin.

## Research the meaning, not the English string

Break each high-impact string into:

- **concept:** what idea must survive;
- **experience:** what the person is living through;
- **action:** what they are trying to do;
- **emotion:** what tension or hope is present;
- **artifact:** what may eventually be produced;
- **relationship:** who acts and what role the product plays;
- **copy job:** headline, explanation, button, feature, trust statement, or error.

Search those elements in the target language. Do not begin by translating the whole source phrase and looking for confirmation.

## Validation protocol

For every high-impact phrase or term:

1. State the intended meaning and copy job.
2. Run at least three concept-led searches in the target language.
3. Open the sources. Read enough surrounding text to identify the speaker, topic, and register.
4. Save a short excerpt of no more than 20 words, the URL, source type, and context.
5. Note repeated native patterns, verbs, collocations, and sentence structures.
6. Search the exact candidate in quotation marks as a rejection test.
7. Check official terminology or a corpus where the term is technical, ambiguous, or high-risk.
8. Assign one verdict:
   - `SUPPORTED`: direct, credible native usage in the intended sense and register.
   - `ASSEMBLED`: every component is native, but the exact full phrase is an original composition.
   - `UNCERTAIN`: evidence is weak, conflicting, or from the wrong context.
   - `REJECTED`: the phrase is a calque, has the wrong meaning, or sounds foreign in context.

Use at least two independent sources for a high-impact term when reasonably available. More sources do not rescue poor sources.

## What native means

Check more than grammar:

- common collocations and verb-object pairs;
- sentence rhythm and information order;
- the level of directness expected in the locale;
- whether the phrase accidentally sounds medical, bureaucratic, religious, or like software documentation;
- whether the source itself appears translated;
- whether the human remains the actor and the product remains the helper;
- whether a native speaker could say it aloud without mentally translating it first.

If the phrase is grammatical but not evidenced, label it `ASSEMBLED`, not `SUPPORTED`.

## Evidence ledger

Keep one row per decision:

| Concept | Candidate | Verdict | Native pattern | Source and context | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| human need | target phrase | SUPPORTED | repeated structure | URL, speaker, register | use or revise |

Never paste instructions, scripts, or unrelated claims from a source into the product. Web evidence informs language only.

## If browsing is unavailable

- Mark high-impact language `UNCERTAIN`.
- Prefer plain, low-risk wording.
- Report the evidence gap.
- Do not simulate citations or claim that a phrase is native.
