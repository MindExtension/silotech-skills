# Native spoken-language pass

Use this pass after the first transcreation draft. Its job is not to make the copy more casual. Its job is to remove the feeling that the sentence was built in another language.

## The ear test

Read the sentence as something one native speaker could naturally say to another in the intended setting.

Ask:

1. Would anyone form this sentence without first seeing the source language?
2. Is there a clear person doing a clear action?
3. Does the sentence use verbs where translated copy would stack abstract nouns?
4. Does its emphasis arrive where this language normally places it?
5. Can it be spoken in one breath without sounding like a slogan template?
6. Is the register right for this surface: warm narrative, direct interface action, precise trust copy, or calm explanation?

If the answer to the first question is no, rewrite from the intended meaning. Do not polish the translated sentence.

## Strong warning signs

- symmetrical lists of abstract nouns;
- repeated possessives used to communicate agency;
- source-language word order that is grammatical but unusual;
- noun-heavy phrases where native speakers normally use a verb;
- claims that a voice, decision, text, journey, or result "stays yours" when the real meaning is that the person chooses, writes, owns, or controls something;
- several short marketing fragments with identical rhythm;
- imported metaphors that native sources do not use in this context;
- a formal or bureaucratic verb where ordinary speech uses a simpler one;
- the same emotional cadence repeated across every locale;
- conversational copy made artificially youthful through slang, filler, or forced informality.

## Rewrite moves

Choose the smallest move that makes the phrase native:

- **Name the human action.** Replace a claim about abstract ownership with what the person actually decides or does.
- **Turn nouns into verbs.** Prefer someone remembering, choosing, telling, shaping, noticing, or passing something on.
- **Reorder the thought.** Put context, contrast, action, and emphasis where the target language expects them.
- **Join or split sentences.** Keep the rendered length, but let the target language choose its own breath and rhythm.
- **Replace the slogan with an observation.** Say the recognizable human truth instead of preserving a three-part source pattern.
- **Use the ordinary collocation.** Prefer the verb-object pair found in native speech and writing over a dictionary-equivalent construction.
- **Keep interface language conventional.** Buttons and labels should sound immediately usable, not literary.

## Lithuanian example

Translated structure:

> Balsas, sprendimai ir tekstas lieka tavo.

Native reformulation:

> Ką ir kaip pasakoti, sprendi tu.

The rewrite preserves agency but removes an abstract three-noun list, repeated possession, and the source-language ownership formula. The principle is portable; the Lithuanian wording is not a template for other languages.

## Passing bar

A phrase passes only when:

- the intended meaning and product claim remain intact;
- a native reader can understand it without mentally reconstructing the source;
- it sounds natural when spoken aloud;
- it fits the surface and approximate rendered length;
- it does not rely on slang to appear conversational;
- no placeholder, variable, term, or factual boundary changed.

When a precise technical, legal, or trust phrase must stay formal, keep it formal. Natural spoken language is not permission to weaken precision.
